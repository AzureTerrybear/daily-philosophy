What it is

Marcus Aurelius kept a private journal that we now call the Meditations. Its Greek title means, roughly, "to himself". This project borrows the idea: philosophy as something you practise in your own notes.

The first season is a Stoic week. Seven short lessons work through the Enchiridion of Epictetus, one idea a day, and each day builds on the one before. By the end you have written seven pages: a small handbook of your own.

There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power. Epictetus, The Enchiridion, §1, translated by Thomas Wentworth Higginson

How a lesson works

  • 01

    Listen

    A two-minute explanation of one passage, with the original text one click away.

  • 02

    Try it

    A short activity applies the idea to an everyday situation and names the common misreadings.

  • 03

    Write your page

    You apply the idea to something from your own day. That page is yours to keep and download.

  • 04

    Carry it

    One small practice to try before the next lesson, which opens by asking how it went.

How it is made

Grounded in the source

Every lesson starts from a passage in a public-domain translation. The explanation is drafted with AI, and each claim in it is tied to the exact words in the passage that support it. An automated check rejects any lesson where those words cannot be found.

Approved by a person

AI can explain Stoicism convincingly and still get it wrong. Anything that comes from outside the passage, such as a detail of Epictetus's life, is flagged separately, and no lesson is published until I have read it against the source and signed it off.

Designed for learning

The week is planned before any lesson is written. Each day targets one common misunderstanding, revisits an earlier idea and follows up on the previous day's practice. There are no points or streaks. The reward is the journal you build.

Still growing

Day 1 is open now and the rest of the Stoic week is on the way, followed by recorded narration and branching scenarios. Later seasons will draw on Marcus Aurelius, Seneca and Plato.
